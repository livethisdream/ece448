reception, including hop-sequence synchronization.}

\vspace{2pt}

This packet is the L\labLesson\ Lab, in three parts, and you turn it in once, complete,
before Lesson 17. Do \textbf{Part 1} on your own before the lab period: run the link
flowgraph and make the predictions you will check at the bench. \textbf{Part 2} is
your own measurements, recorded in class: Questions 3 to 5 on your own laptop, and
Question 6 at a PlutoSDR station with your team. Do \textbf{Part 3} on your own after
the lab: compare the measurements with the predictions and answer the analysis
questions.

\textbf{Grading.} You submit the packet once, we grade it against Objective 6.5 on
the course's 1 / 0 scale, and you may revise it under course policy. Paste screenshots where a
question asks for one.

\textbf{Transmitting.} In class we transmit only into a cable, into a shielded
enclosure, or at very low power in an ISM band, and only when the lesson says to.
Questions 1 to 5 run on your laptop and transmit nothing. Question 6 transmits from a
PlutoSDR's TX port, through a cable with an inline 30 dB SMA attenuator, to the same
Pluto's RX port, at 915 MHz. Connect the cable and attenuator before you run the
flowgraph, and never connect an antenna in this lab. Never set TX attenuation below
30 dB or change the slider's range; the RX input is rated to only a few dBm. We have
run the PlutoSDR flowgraph only in simulation, so Question 6 is its first run on
hardware.

The flowgraphs are on the L16 lesson page, \texttt{L16-fhss-tx.grc} and
\texttt{L16-fhss-link.grc}, and on the L16 Lab page, \texttt{L16-fhss-pluto.grc}. Every
number below assumes their settings: Lesson 8's FSK at 1000 baud, sampled at 128 kHz;
one packet per hop, made of \texttt{guard} guard bytes, the 12-byte access code and
header, the 14-byte payload \texttt{USAFA ECE 448} with its newline, and
\texttt{guard} more guard bytes; $N$ channels 10 kHz apart; and key 448, whose sync
channel for $N = 8$ is channel 7. With one channel of $N$ jammed, the fraction of
packets delivered is about
\[ P = 1 - \frac{1}{N}. \]

\question \textbf{Predictions.} You will check each of these at the bench.

\begin{parts}

	\part Set \texttt{guard} to 4 bytes. Find the bits in one hop, the time on one hop,
	the hop rate, the time for one cycle of 8 channels, and the samples in one hop at
	128 kHz.

	\begin{center}
	\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
	\begin{tabular}{| l | K{0.75in} | K{0.75in} | K{0.75in} | K{0.75in} | K{0.85in} |}
	\hline
	& Bits per hop & Hop time & Hop rate & Cycle & Samples \\\hline
	\texttt{guard} = 4 & \kv{272} & \kv{272 ms} & \kv{3.68 /s} & \kv{2.18 s} & \kv{34{,}816} \\\hline
	\end{tabular}
	\end{center}

		\begin{solution}[0.9in]
		\eq{ n &= 8 \times (4 + 12 + 14 + 4) = 272\ \text{bits}, \quad T_\text{hop} = 272/1000 = 272\ \text{ms} \\
		T_\text{cycle} &= 8 \times 272\ \text{ms} = 2.18\ \text{s}, \quad 128{,}000 \times 0.272\ \text{s} = 34{,}816\ \text{samples} }
		\end{solution}

	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.58\linewidth}
		\part With \texttt{guard} = 4, about how early and how late can the receiver change
		channel and still receive every packet?
		\begin{solution}[0.8in]
		Each guard is 32 bits, 32 ms at 1000 baud, so the receiver can change channel about
		32 ms early or 32 ms late.
		\end{solution}
	\end{minipage}\hfill%
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.37\linewidth}
		\raggedleft \vspace{12pt}
		\ansbox[1.1in]{about $\pm 32$ ms}
	\end{minipage}

	\part For the hopper you build in Question 3, choose your own order of the 8
	channels, using each once, and write the hop frequencies in that order. With
	\texttt{dwell} = 32{,}000 samples at 128 kHz, find the time on one hop and the time
	for one cycle.
		\begin{solution}[1.0in]
		The answer depends on the order. For 3, 0, 6, 1, 7, 4, 2, 5 the frequencies are
		$-5$, $-35$, $+25$, $-25$, $+35$, $+5$, $-15$, and $+15$ kHz, from
		$f_k = 10\ \text{kHz} \times (k - 3.5)$. One hop lasts $32{,}000/128{,}000 = 250$ ms,
		and a cycle of 8 hops 2.0 s.
		\end{solution}

	\part A jammer strong enough to take every hop on its channel sits on one channel
	that is not the sync channel. Predict the fraction of packets delivered with 2 and
	with 4 channels. Then, for a weaker jammer that lets a fraction $p$ through when the
	link stays on its channel, write the fraction delivered when the link hops over 8
	channels.

	\begin{center}
	\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
	\begin{tabular}{| l | K{1.0in} | K{1.0in} | K{1.6in} |}
	\hline
	Channels & 2 & 4 & 8, weak jammer \\\hline
	Delivered & \kv{50\%} & \kv{75\%} & \kv{$1 - (1-p)/8$} \\\hline
	\end{tabular}
	\end{center}

		\begin{solution}[0.6in]
		$1 - 1/2 = 50\%$ and $1 - 1/4 = 75\%$. With 8 channels the jammer reaches 1 hop in
		8, and loses $1 - p$ of those, so $P = 1 - (1-p)/8$.
		\end{solution}

\end{parts}

		The Transmitted band waterfall shows the carrier stepping through the 8 channels in
		the chosen order, 250 ms on each, with the jammer as a steady line at $+5$ kHz. The
		De-hopped waterfall shows the carrier as a steady line at 0 Hz and the jammer as
		short dashes that move every 250 ms, at $5\ \text{kHz} - f_k$.
		\end{solution}

	\part Paste a screenshot with the Delay block set to \texttt{dwell}. Explain what
	the De-hopped waterfall shows.
		\begin{solution}[1.4in]
		The receiver's synthesizer is now one hop late, so on each hop it removes the
		previous hop's frequency. The carrier appears at $f_k - f_{k-1}$, a different
		frequency on every hop and never 0 Hz, because consecutive channels always differ.
		A receiver tuned this way would receive nothing.
		\end{solution}

	\part Describe the De-hopped waterfall with the delay at 2000 samples.
		\begin{solution}[1.0in]
		2000 samples is 15.6 ms, about 6\% of a hop. The carrier sits at 0 Hz for most of
		each hop, and for the first 15.6 ms of each hop it jumps to $f_k - f_{k-1}$, which shows
		as a short blip at each hop boundary. A receiver with 16 ms guards would still
		receive every packet.
		\end{solution}

\end{parts}

\fullwidth{\large\textbf{Part 3: Writeup} \normalsize(individual, after the lab)}

\question \textbf{Analysis.}

\begin{parts}

	\part Compare your jamming table with Question 2(d). Use your De-hopped waterfall
	from Question 3(a) to explain why a jammer stronger than the signal takes only the
	hops on its own channel, and why raising its power further does not take more.
		\begin{solution}[1.5in]
		The measurements followed $1 - 1/N$: about 50\%, 75\%, and 88\% for 2, 4, and 8
		channels. After de-hopping, the wanted signal sits at 0 Hz on every hop, but the
		jammer moves to $f_\text{jam} - f_k$, which lies inside the receiver's $\pm 2$ kHz
		filter only on the hops tuned to the jammer's channel. On every other hop the filter
		removes it however strong it is, so more power takes no more hops; it only makes
		sure the hops on its own channel are lost.
		\end{solution}

	\part Compare your timing windows with 2 and 4 guard bytes. Find the share of each
	hop that carries the payload in each case, and explain the tradeoff.
		\begin{solution}[1.5in]
		With 2 guard bytes the window was about $-16$ to $+18$ ms; with 4 it was about
		$-31$ to $+34$ ms, twice as wide. The payload is 112 bits of $240$, 47\%, with
		2 guard bytes, and 112 of $272$, 41\%, with 4, and the guard bits take 13\% and
		24\% of each hop. More guard tolerates a receiver with worse timing, or a synthesizer
		that takes longer to settle, but each hop carries less data and the link's
		throughput falls.
		\end{solution}

	\part From Question 4(d), explain why one jammed channel can keep a receiver from
	ever locking although it costs a locked receiver only 1 hop in 8, and propose a
	change to Hop Sync or to the packet that removes that weakness.
		\begin{solution}[1.5in]
		Hop Sync acquires only on the sync channel, by the jump in its power when hop 0
		arrives. A jammer already there hides that jump, so the receiver waits forever; a
		receiver already locked keeps its timing from the packets on the other seven
		channels. Two fixes: put the hop number in every packet's header and let Hop Sync
		park on any channel, lock on the first packet it decodes there, and read which hop
		it was; or give the receiver two or more sync channels and have it move to the
		next when one stays busy, as Bluetooth's inquiry procedure uses 32.
		\end{solution}

\end{parts}
