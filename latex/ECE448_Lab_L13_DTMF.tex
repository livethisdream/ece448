signal.}

\vspace{2pt}

This packet is the L\labLesson\ Lab, in three parts, and you turn it in once, complete,
before Lesson 14. Do \textbf{Part 1} on your own before the lab period: run the receiver
and make the predictions you will check at the bench. \textbf{Part 2} is your own
measurements, recorded in class on your own laptop. Do \textbf{Part 3} on your own after
the lab: compare the measurements with the predictions and the standard, and answer the
analysis questions.

\textbf{Grading.} One submission, graded against Objective 6.2 on the course's
1 / 0 scale, with revision allowed under course policy. Paste screenshots where a
question asks for one.

\textbf{Transmitting.} Nothing in this lab transmits. Question 7 uses your phone's
speaker and your laptop's microphone.

The flowgraphs are on the L13 lesson page, \texttt{L13-dtmf-rx.grc},
\texttt{L13-dtmf-link.grc}, and \texttt{L13-dtmf-keypad.grc}, and on the L13 Lab page,
\texttt{L13-dtmf-mic.grc}, with the captures \texttt{L13-capture.wav} and
\texttt{L13-lab-capture.wav}. Both captures are synthetic: \texttt{L13-dtmf-capture.grc}
dialed them and added noise. Every number below assumes the flowgraphs' settings:
$f_s = 8$ kHz, Goertzel blocks of $N = 205$ samples unless a question changes $N$, and the
decoder's rules from the lesson: the best row and column bins hold 35\% of a block's
energy, each beats its group by 6 dB, the twist is within $-10$ to $+6$ dB, and three

\question \textbf{A real phone.} Run \texttt{L13-dtmf-mic.grc} as the lab page lists.

\begin{parts}

	\part Record the number you pressed and the Digits label, and paste a screenshot of the
	window while you press one key.
		\begin{solution}[1.5in]
		Answers vary. With the phone close and keys pressed slowly, the decoder reads
		every key. The bins rise and fall less cleanly than in the synthetic captures,
		because the room adds echo and noise, and the phone and microphone do not pass
		every frequency equally, which adds twist.
		\end{solution}

	\part Press keys as fast as you can. What does the decoder miss?
		\begin{solution}[0.9in]
		Answers vary. Most phones play a tone for as long as a key is held, so
		fast presses make short tones and short gaps; a tone shorter than about 33 ms is
		missed, and a repeated key with a gap shorter than about 20 ms counts once.
		\end{solution}

	\part Talk, whistle, or play music near the microphone. Record any false digits.
		\begin{solution}[0.9in]
		Answers vary; speech should produce few or none. A whistle is one tone, so it
		fails the test that needs one row and one column tone. Music with two steady
		notes near a row and a column frequency can produce a false digit.
		\end{solution}

\end{parts}

\fullwidth{\large\textbf{Part 3: Writeup} \normalsize(individual, after the lab)}

\question \textbf{Analysis.}

\begin{parts}

	\part Compare the twist, tone, and gap limits you measured in Question 5 with the
	standard's rules from the lesson. Does the decoder meet each rule?
		\begin{solution}[1.5in]
		Twist: the standard requires $-8$ to $+4$ dB, and the decoder accepted $-9$ to
		$+5$ dB, so it meets the rule with about 1 dB to spare. Tone: it must accept 40 ms
		and reject anything under 23 ms; the decoder accepted every 35 ms tone and none
		at 28 ms, so it meets both. Gap: it must work with 40 ms gaps; the decoder needed
		only about 25 ms, so it meets that too.
		\end{solution}

	\part Use Question 6 to explain what a shorter and a longer Goertzel block gain and
	lose, and why $N = 205$ is the usual choice at 8 kHz.
		\begin{solution}[1.5in]
		A short block responds quickly, so it catches short tones, but its bins are wide,
		78 Hz at $N = 102$, so it accepts tones too far off frequency and short bursts the
		standard says to reject. A long block has narrow bins, 19.5 Hz at $N = 410$, but
		lasts 51 ms, longer than a legal tone, and rejects tones the standard allows to
		be 1.5\% off. $N = 205$ gives 39 Hz bins and a 25.6 ms block, which meets the
		timing and frequency rules at once.
		\end{solution}

	\part Compare the phone with the synthetic captures, and propose one change to the
	decoder for a noisy room.
		\begin{solution}[1.5in]
		Answers vary. The phone's bins are lower and rougher, with more twist and more
		energy outside the eight bins. Reasonable changes: require an absolute level as
		well as the 35\% share, so quiet background sounds cannot trigger a digit;
		require four blocks instead of three for more protection against speech, at the
		cost of the shortest tones; or band-pass the audio to 600 to 1700 Hz first.
		\end{solution}

\end{parts}

\end{questions}
